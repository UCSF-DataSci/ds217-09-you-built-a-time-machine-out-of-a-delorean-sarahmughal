
Missing Data Report

1. Missing Value Summary
In total, the missing value count was 323, accounting for roughly 96.4% of the dataset. This isn’t surprising since expanding monthly data into daily data creates far more dates than original measurements.

2. Missing Data Patterns
The missingness followed a very predictable pattern: every month-end date had a real recorded temperature, while all the other days in between were empty. There wasn’t any unusual behavior tied to certain months, days of the week, or seasons—it was simply a result of how the data was resampled. Because of this, the missing values didn’t represent errors or sensor failures but were instead an artifact of changing the time granularity.

3. Imputation Method
To handle the missing values, I used a rolling mean with a 3-day window. I also tried forward fill, backward fill, and interpolation, but the rolling mean felt like the best balance between smoothness and realism. It avoids the “flat-line” effect that forward fill creates and doesn’t rely too heavily on future data like backward fill does.

4. Rationale
The rolling mean works well here because temperature tends to change gradually rather than jumping sharply from one day to the next. Using neighboring values to fill gaps gives a smoother and more natural-looking time series. It also reduces the risk of introducing unrealistic spikes.

5. Pros and Cons
The main advantage is that rolling averages are easy to interpret and produce stable, smoothed values. The downside is that they can slightly blur real peaks or dips, especially with a small window size. Still, for this dataset, the trade-off felt reasonable.

6. Example
Before imputation, a typical stretch looked like: [98.777, NaN, NaN, NaN]. After applying the rolling mean, it became something like: [98.777, 98.777, 98.777, NaN] because I chose a window of 3, which fills the gaps without creating abrupt jumps, but also doesn't over-smooth the data.
